MBSE & System Architecture: Automotive Cooling System

Model-Based Systems Engineering of an automotive cooling system in CATIA Magic: a single executable SysML model linking stakeholder scenarios to verifiable requirements, functional architecture and physical components, with automated design-verification workflows.

Overview

As the systems-engineering project of the Model-based Product Development course (MSE — Institute for Machine Elements and System Development) during my Erasmus semester at RWTH Aachen, I developed the complete MBSE architecture of an automotive cooling system. The vehicle is offered in three performance classes (135 / 185 / 250 kW) and has to serve conflicting usage scenarios — city commuting and alpine long-distance trips — under strict thermal and spatial constraints. The use case follows the cooling-system benchmark published by BMW (Höpfner et al., 2021) that forms the backbone of the course.

The objective was a single, fully integrated SysML model in CATIA Magic (Cameo Systems Modeler) that links customer requirements directly to functional structures and physical component geometries — so that component suitability is verified automatically upon parameter change, instead of through manual iteration on documents and spreadsheets.

Requirements Engineering

The two usage scenarios were first translated into boundary conditions and then into verifiable requirements: the engine temperature must stay within 80–120 °C for every power class; the pump speed must not exceed 8,000 rpm; the impeller must fit the installation space of the engine compartment; and, wherever possible, the same components must be shared across the three performance classes (purchasing constraint).

Each requirement was modelled as a SysML requirement block and traced (satisfy / verify) to the functions and components that realize and check it. The parameter space of the system model acts as the single point of truth (SPOT): every diagram and every workflow reads the same, unambiguous parameter set, eliminating the redundancy of document-centred development.

Functional & Structural Architecture

The functional architecture was decomposed with Block Definition Diagrams (BDD) and detailed with Internal Block Diagrams (IBD), distinguishing the three flow types of a technical system: mechanical energy input, coolant volume flow and control signals. This made explicit which subsystems have a functional influence on the engine temperature — the property that ultimately drives the design.

Behaviour was modelled with Activity diagrams for the calculation workflows (thermal balance of the circuit → required volume flow → pump operating point) and Sequence diagrams for the interaction between system elements and the automated verification workflow. On the structural side, the solution context (vehicle, engine unit) and the solution structure (cooling circuit, water pump, roller bearing) were modelled as parameterized blocks connected across the SysML hierarchy.

Solution Space: Morphological Box

Alternative technologies for transferring mechanical power to the fluid were explored systematically through a morphological box: centrifugal and axial-flow concepts were benchmarked against displacement-type alternatives (external gear pump, axial piston pump), each characterized by its own flow law — displacement machines deliver V̇ = n · V0, while rotodynamic machines follow their pressure–flow characteristic.

The candidates were assessed against the requirements — operating point, speed limit, installation space and controllability across the three power classes. The centrifugal pump with a parameterized impeller (width as design variable) emerged as the preferred concept and became the physical architecture verified by the workflows below.

Automated Verification Workflows

The model was made executable and parameterized: workflows orchestrated through the Activity and Sequence diagrams run the verification automatically for each power class. Starting from the engine's thermal load, the workflow computes the required coolant volume flow, scales the pump operating point across the classes, and checks — without manual intervention — that the engine temperature stays within the [80, 120] °C window, that the impeller speed remains ≤ 8,000 rpm, and that the impeller width keeps the pump inside its installation space. Design adaptations act on both levers of the benchmark: the software side (control resistance) and the mechanics side (impeller width), matching the variant logic of the reference use case.

Results

  • Delivered an executable, parameterized SysML system model of the cooling system, built entirely in CATIA Magic.
  • Automated verification of component suitability against thermodynamic requirements and spatial/production constraints for all three engine power classes (135 / 185 / 250 kW).
  • Full traceability from stakeholder scenarios to requirements, functions, components and verification links — within one model, not across disconnected documents.
  • Technology selection (centrifugal vs. axial-flow and displacement alternatives) documented in-model through the morphological box, with the trade-off logic reproducible.
  • Removed manual iteration from the design-selection loop: any parameter change re-triggers the verification workflow and immediately flags violated requirements, significantly streamlining the selection of the pump design.
