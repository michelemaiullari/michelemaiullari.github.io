Tech Startup Project — GenAir · Role: Aerospace / CFD Engineer

AI-Driven Aerodynamic Design & CFD Validation Platform

A generative-AI platform for aerodynamic design: an inverse neural model proposes airfoil and control-surface geometries from target aerodynamic requirements, automated high-fidelity CFD pipelines validate them against those targets, and an adjoint-based fine-tuning architecture is designed to feed the flow solver's sensitivities directly back into the network — replacing manual CFD iteration with a closed IA–CFD loop for wings and control surfaces.

Confidentiality notice. This project was developed in a tech-startup context involving external aerospace stakeholders. The identity of the industrial partner, the full technical dossier and quantitative results are subject to confidentiality and cannot be published. The architecture, methodology and roadmap below are described at a level of detail compatible with those obligations.

Overview

The aerodynamic design of wings and control surfaces still runs on a manually-driven loop: every geometry variant means a new domain, a new mesh, a new case setup and a new convergence watch — days of engineering time per iteration, and a hard ceiling on how many candidates a team can afford to explore. GenAir, the tech startup where I worked as Aerospace/CFD engineer, was built on a different workflow: let a generative model propose geometries already consistent with the target aerodynamic coefficients, and concentrate CFD where it creates value — verification, validation and physical refinement.

I owned the CFD and physical-modelling side of the platform end-to-end: solver configuration, automated validation pipelines, the aerodynamic evaluation of everything the model generates, and the design of the physics-informed fine-tuning strategy. The work brought me into direct technical discussions with leading aerospace players — Embraer among them — and with the DTA aerospace technology district, and I authored the complete technical roadmap for the platform's industrial evolution, drafted for a leading international aerospace group.

Starting Point: Feasibility Proven, Physics Missing

The starting prototype had already demonstrated the core feasibility: a supervised inverse model learning the map from aerodynamic parameters and polar data to a 2D airfoil, with a panel method as a fast validator. Solid as a demonstrator, it carried three structural limits for any industrial domain:

  • A physically blind validator. Panel methods assume incompressible, inviscid flow: no compressibility, no shock waves, no viscous drag — acceptable for subsonic screening, unacceptable where the real operating regime lives.
  • A subsonic, strictly 2D world. Training data and generated geometry were both confined to 2D sections at low Mach numbers.
  • An open loop. Nothing fed what validation learned back into the model: generation and physics never talked to each other.

Platform Architecture

I structured the platform as eight distinct layers, each replaceable without touching the others — the precondition for evolving from prototype to industrial tool:

  • Data layer — airfoil geometries, polar data, operating conditions and CFD results, normalised for training, benchmarking and retraining.
  • Geometric representation — continuous parametrisations (PARSEC, CST, Bézier/B-spline, FFD) chosen for smoothness, constraint control and mesh compatibility.
  • Inverse neural model — the core: aerodynamic requirements → candidate geometry, with alternative variants and a confidence score.
  • CFD validation layer — automated case generation, meshing, solve, coefficient extraction and convergence tracking.
  • Physical fine-tuning layer — gradient-free baseline with an adjoint-based evolution path.
  • 3D geometry generation — section stacking with twist, sweep and taper, evolving towards direct 3D generation.
  • Frontend / API layer — a web application orchestrating generation, parametric editing, exports and CFD runs.
  • Experiment tracking — every run registered with its model, dataset, solver and mesh configuration: reproducible, auditable output.

Solver Chain: From Incompressible Baseline to Shock-Capturing

I configured the validation chain on two physical tiers. The incompressible solver covers the fast tier: screening candidates and supporting the training-data pipeline where compressibility effects are negligible. The compressible tier is where physical fidelity becomes non-negotiable: variable density, pressure–density–temperature coupling and the energy equation, conservative formulations of the governing equations, and shock-capturing numerical schemes with Riemann-solver-based fluxes to handle shock waves and the steep pressure gradients of transonic and supersonic regimes, with RANS turbulence models consistent with the operating regime and the wall conditions.

The panel method was deliberately demoted, not deleted: it remains a preliminary screening validator for quick 2D subsonic tests, never the final word on any geometry. That division of labour — cheap screening, expensive verification — is what keeps the loop fast without lying about the physics. The shock-capturing fundamentals behind this tier are the same I implemented from scratch in Fortran in my shock-wave CFD solver project.

Automated CFD Pipelines

I developed automated CFD pipelines that take any geometry the model generates through the entire chain unattended: computational domain, mesh generation, boundary conditions, solve, extraction of lift, drag and moment coefficients and of pressure distributions, and convergence checks. Every outcome is registered into the dataset — including non-convergent or physically implausible geometries, logged as such. Treating failures as data is what makes the IA–CFD pipeline more robust with every iteration, instead of silently biasing the training set towards the cases that happen to converge.

Physics-Informed Fine-Tuning: Black-Box Now, Adjoint Next

The layer that turns the platform from a generator into a learning system is physical fine-tuning — closing the loop between what the flow solver measures and what the network generates. I implemented it at two levels and architected the third:

  • Supervised pre-training. The model learns from dataset pairs: aerodynamic parameters → reference geometry.
  • Black-box fine-tuning — implemented. Generated geometries are scored by a fast aerodynamic evaluator against an aerodynamic loss with geometric and smoothness penalties; a gradient-free estimator updates the model without backpropagating through the solver. Computationally cheap, and the working baseline.
  • Adjoint-based fine-tuning — architecture defined by me; end-to-end implementation scheduled. The CFD solver solves the discrete adjoint problem and returns the sensitivities of the objective with respect to the geometry; the network computes how the geometry depends on its own weights; the two gradients compose into a single update.

In formula terms: the solver supplies ∂L/∂geometry, the network propagates ∂geometry/∂weights, and the weight update follows the chain product of the two — a true physical gradient reaching the network's weights, with no surrogate model standing between the flow solver and the training loop. This is the transition I designed to move the platform from gradient-free tuning to physics-informed optimisation.

Extending to 3D Control Surfaces

Control surfaces are three-dimensional objects, so the architecture extends generation from 2D sections to 3D surfaces in two stages. Stage one is geometric: the inverse model keeps generating 2D sections, and a dedicated module stacks root and tip sections along the span — first along a straight stacking axis, then along a curved guide — interpolating intermediate sections and managing twist, sweep, taper and dihedral, smoothing the surface and exporting it mesh-ready for volume-mesh generation and finite-volume CFD 3D. Stage two is evolutionary: direct generation of 3D parametric geometries by the model itself, trained and refined on CFD 3D results. At the end of my contribution the proof-of-concept architecture was structurally capable of predicting and validating 3D control surfaces, while direct 3D generation remains a roadmap objective, tracked as such below.

Status and Roadmap

I structured the industrial evolution as a phased roadmap. Status at the end of my contribution:

  • Supervised inverse model (parameters → 2D airfoil) — implemented
  • Panel-method preliminary validator (screening only) — implemented
  • Black-box physical fine-tuning — implemented
  • Automated 2D CFD pipeline with convergence handling — implemented
  • Incompressible + compressible solver chain (shock-capturing, RANS) — configured, integrated in the validation pipeline
  • Adjoint-based fine-tuning (CFD sensitivities → network weights) — architecture defined; end-to-end implementation scheduled
  • 3D generation pipeline (section stacking → direct generation) — architecture defined, proof-of-concept level
  • Client-domain dataset and supersonic retraining — planned
  • Wind-tunnel experimental validation — outside the initial scope

Keeping implemented and planned work visibly separate is deliberate: the platform's value proposition stands on what already runs, and the roadmap shows where it goes next.

Results

  • Delivered a working proof-of-concept IA–CFD architecture: from target aerodynamic coefficients to a generated airfoil, through automated CFD validation — demonstrated to industry leaders.
  • Replaced simplified panel-method validation with physically robust CFD pipelines capable of capturing shock waves and high pressure gradients — the precondition for training data consistent with the real operating regime.
  • Designed the transition from gradient-free black-box fine-tuning to adjoint-based physical fine-tuning, linking CFD sensitivities directly to the neural network's weights.
  • Authored the complete technical roadmap for the platform's industrial evolution, drafted for a leading international aerospace group, with direct exposure to Embraer and the DTA aerospace technology district.
  • Substantially compressed the design cycle: engineering time shifts from running manual CFD iterations to judging validated candidate geometries.
